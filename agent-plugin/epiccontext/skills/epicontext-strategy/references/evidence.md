Cited data points from trusted sources for strategy decisions.

## Strategy as choice

### EVD-STR-001 — Strategy is making explicit choices about what NOT to do
- **Claim:** Porter: strategy is fundamentally about deliberately choosing different activities to deliver a unique mix of value. The essence is choosing what NOT to do.
- **Source:** Michael Porter, *What is Strategy?* (HBR, 1996)
- **Citation:** [HBR — What is Strategy?](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy)
- **Used in:** strategy

### EVD-STR-002 — Strategy as a cascade of five choices
- **Claim:** Roger Martin's framework: every strategy answers (1) What is our winning aspiration? (2) Where will we play? (3) How will we win? (4) What capabilities must be in place? (5) What management systems are required?
- **Source:** Roger Martin & A.G. Lafley, *Playing to Win* (2013)
- **Citation:** Lafley & Martin, *Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works*
- **Used in:** strategy

### EVD-STR-003 — Good strategy has diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action
- **Claim:** Rumelt: bad strategy is goals + ambition + slogans. Good strategy has three parts: a diagnosis of the situation, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions designed to carry out the policy.
- **Source:** Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* (2011)
- **Citation:** Rumelt, *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters*
- **Used in:** strategy

### EVD-STR-004 — Strategy without context is broken
- **Claim:** Wardley Mapping: strategy decisions made without situational awareness (where components are in their evolution from genesis to commodity) systematically fail. The map provides the context strategy operates within.
- **Source:** Simon Wardley, *Wardley Mapping* (open-license, ongoing)
- **Citation:** [Wardley Maps](https://learnwardleymapping.com/)
- **Used in:** strategy, decisions/build-vs-buy-vs-partner

## Business model

### EVD-STR-005 — Business Model Canvas: nine building blocks
- **Claim:** Osterwalder's framework: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships, Cost Structure. Together these describe how a business creates, delivers, and captures value.
- **Source:** Alexander Osterwalder & Yves Pigneur, *Business Model Generation* (2010)
- **Citation:** Osterwalder, *Business Model Generation*
- **Used in:** business-model

### EVD-STR-006 — Lean Canvas adapts BMC for early-stage
- **Claim:** Maurya's adaptation replaces Key Activities, Key Resources, Customer Relationships, and Key Partners with Problem, Solution, Key Metrics, and Unfair Advantage — fitting earlier-stage businesses where customer development matters more than partnerships.
- **Source:** Ash Maurya, *Running Lean* (2012)
- **Citation:** Maurya, *Running Lean: Iterate from Plan A to a Plan That Works*
- **Used in:** business-model

## OGSM and operating frames

### EVD-STR-007 — OGSM caps for operability
- **Claim:** OGSM practitioner literature consistently recommends keeping the framework tight: typically one Objective per OGSM plan, 3-6 Goals (concrete and quantitative), 3-6 Strategies, and 3-6 Measures. Wider OGSMs become wishlists that don't operationalize.
- **Source:** OGSM practitioner literature (P&G adoption, ArchPoint Consulting, Perdoo, Thrive Sparrow); Wikipedia consolidates the framework
- **Citation:** [Wikipedia — OGSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OGSM); [ArchPoint Consulting — OGSM Strategy Guide](https://archpointconsulting.com/ogsm-strategy-guide); [Perdoo — OGSM Guide](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/ogsm)
- **Caveat:** OGSM has no formal academic publications — it spread through corporate practice (Japan post-WWII, then P&G). Specific numerical caps are practitioner consensus rather than research-backed; treat as directional principle.
- **Origin note:** OGSM originated in 1950s Japan as part of post-WWII corporate planning; P&G adopted and popularized it from the 1990s, integrating it across their global operations.
- **Used in:** ogsm

### EVD-STR-008 — Goals must be measurable and time-bound
- **Claim:** Doerr's OKR framework (and OGSM, KPI literature) consistently finds that goals without numbers and dates don't drive execution. Vague goals produce vague work.
- **Source:** John Doerr, *Measure What Matters*; OKR practitioner literature
- **Citation:** Doerr, *Measure What Matters*
- **Used in:** ogsm

## Decision quality

### EVD-STR-009 — Decision quality vs. outcome quality
- **Claim:** Duke: a good decision can have a bad outcome (variance) and a bad decision can have a good outcome (luck). Evaluate decisions by the quality of the reasoning at the time, not the outcome alone.
- **Source:** Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (2018)
- **Citation:** Duke, *Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts*
- **Used in:** business-case

### EVD-STR-010 — Premortems improve decision quality
- **Claim:** Klein's research: imagining the failure of a project BEFORE making the decision (premortem) surfaces risks that prospective hindsight misses. Adopted by Kahneman as a debiasing technique.
- **Source:** Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem" (HBR, 2007); Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*
- **Citation:** [HBR — Performing a Project Premortem](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)
- **Used in:** business-case, decisions/now-vs-later

## Roadmaps

### EVD-STR-011 — Outcome-focused roadmaps outperform feature-focused
- **Claim:** Cagan's empirical work across product organizations: roadmaps organized by desired outcomes (with features as candidate experiments) outperform feature-list roadmaps in both shipping the right thing and team motivation.
- **Source:** Marty Cagan, *Inspired*, *Empowered*
- **Citation:** Cagan, *Inspired: How to Create Tech Products Customers Love*
- **Used in:** roadmap

### EVD-STR-012 — Now/Next/Later as confidence calibration
- **Claim:** Bastow's framework: Now/Next/Later horizons capture both sequence and confidence. Items in Now are committed; Next are likely; Later are directional. Reduces the false certainty of dated roadmaps.
- **Source:** Janna Bastow, ProdPad practitioner work
- **Citation:** [ProdPad — Now Next Later Roadmap](https://www.prodpad.com/blog/now-next-later-roadmap/)
- **Used in:** roadmap

### EVD-STR-013 — Geoffrey Moore positioning statement
- **Claim:** Moore's positioning template — "For [target customer] who [statement of need], the [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitive alternative], our product [primary differentiation]" — predates Dunford and remains a widely-used scaffold. Especially well-suited to crossing-the-chasm contexts (early adopter to early majority transition) where category clarity and beachhead segment matter more than nuanced differentiation.
- **Source:** Geoffrey Moore — Chasm Group; multiple books
- **Citation:** Moore, G.A. (2014). *Crossing the Chasm: Marketing and Selling Disruptive Products to Mainstream Customers* (3rd ed.). HarperBusiness.
- **Caveat:** Moore's structure is more rigid than Dunford's; Dunford built on Moore's work and added flexibility around competitive alternatives and "so what" testing of differentiated value.
- **Used in:** strategy, positioning (Brand group)

### EVD-STR-014 — Porter's Five Forces for industry analysis
- **Claim:** Porter's Five Forces (1979) provides a structured analysis of industry attractiveness via five competitive forces: rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers. The framework remains foundational for industry-level strategy and is taught as standard at every major business school.
- **Source:** Michael Porter — Harvard Business School
- **Citation:** Porter, M.E. (1979). "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy." *Harvard Business Review*. Updated as Porter, M.E. (2008). "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy." *Harvard Business Review*, January 2008.
- **Caveat:** Static framework; industries change faster than Porter's model assumes. Best used as a snapshot input to broader strategic analysis, not a one-time deliverable.
- **Used in:** strategy, business-case, which-strategy-framework

### EVD-STR-015 — Wardley Mapping for situational awareness
- **Claim:** Wardley Mapping plots user needs (top) and the components that serve them along two axes: value chain (vertical) and evolution stage (horizontal: genesis → custom-built → product → commodity/utility). Reveals what to build vs buy, where to focus, and how the landscape evolves over time. Particularly valuable for tech-heavy strategies where component evolution materially affects competitive position.
- **Source:** Simon Wardley — independent practitioner
- **Citation:** Wardley, S. (2018). *Wardley Mapping: Topographical Intelligence in Business*. Available free under Creative Commons via Medium and learnwardleymapping.com.
- **Caveat:** Steep learning curve. Mapping is the easier part; making decisions from a map requires practice. Most practitioners use it as a thinking aid more than a deliverable.
- **Used in:** strategy, build-vs-buy-vs-partner, architecture (Build group)

### EVD-STR-016 — Ansoff Matrix for growth strategy
- **Claim:** Igor Ansoff's matrix (1957) frames growth as four choices on a 2×2 of market (existing/new) × product (existing/new): market penetration (existing both, lowest risk), market development (new market, existing product), product development (existing market, new product), and diversification (new both, highest risk). Foundational to growth strategy thinking; appears in nearly every strategy textbook.
- **Source:** Igor Ansoff — *Harvard Business Review*; later books
- **Citation:** Ansoff, H.I. (1957). "Strategies for Diversification." *Harvard Business Review*, 35(5), 113-124. Refined in Ansoff, H.I. (1965). *Corporate Strategy*.
- **Caveat:** The 2×2 simplification hides important detail (e.g., adjacent markets vs. genuinely new). Use as a discussion frame, not as the analysis itself.
- **Used in:** strategy, focus-vs-expand, which-strategy-framework
