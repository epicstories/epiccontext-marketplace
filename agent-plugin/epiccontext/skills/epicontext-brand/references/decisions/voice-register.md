# Decision Tree: Voice Register

Picking the right voice register: formal vs casual, warm vs direct, expert vs peer.

## Start here

**Who is the reader?**

```
├─ A working professional in a healthcare-adjacent context
│   → Lean: warm, direct, professional (not clinical, not luxury)
│
├─ An enterprise B2B buyer evaluating tools
│   → Lean: confident, specific, expert (not casual, not promotional)
│
├─ A consumer making an emotional purchase
│   → Lean: warm, personable, evocative (without sliding into superlatives)
│
├─ A developer reading API documentation
│   → Lean: direct, precise, no-fluff (but not unfriendly)
│
└─ A government / institutional reader
    → Lean: clear, formal, plain-language (not bureaucratic, not casual)
```

## Three axes that matter

### Formal ↔ Casual

**Formal:** "We will confirm your booking via email."
**Casual:** "We'll send you a confirmation email."
**Mid:** "Your confirmation arrives by email."

Most consumer products live in the casual-to-mid range. Most B2B sits mid. Government, legal, finance often need formal.

### Warm ↔ Cool

**Warm:** "Welcome back. Lisa's looking forward to your appointment."
**Cool:** "Booking confirmed. Tuesday, 2pm with Lisa."
**Mid:** "Your booking with Lisa is confirmed for Tuesday, 2pm."

Service businesses lean warmer; technical/professional often cooler. Calibrate by surface — confirmations can be cooler than support responses.

### Expert ↔ Peer

**Expert:** "We recommend monthly visits for sustained results based on tissue recovery patterns."
**Peer:** "Most regulars find monthly works best — your therapist will help you figure out what's right for you."
**Mid:** "Most customers see best results from monthly visits. We can adjust based on what works for you."

Healthcare and finance often lean expert (authority matters). Lifestyle and consumer often lean peer (relatability matters). B2B varies by buyer.

## Common mistakes

**Trying to be everything**: "warm AND authoritative AND playful AND serious" doesn't compose. Pick.

**Voice that contradicts product reality**: a casual voice on a formal product (legal services, financial tools) breaks trust. A formal voice on a casual product (consumer apps, lifestyle brands) feels stiff.

**Voice that doesn't match audience expectations**: an expert voice when the audience is uncertain creates intimidation; a peer voice when the audience needs authority creates doubt.

## Calibration by surface

Same brand voice, calibrated for surface:

- **In-app**: Most direct. Brevity rules. Less warmth needed (the product is the warmth).
- **Marketing email**: More energy. Slightly more promotional language allowed (still within voice).
- **Transactional email**: Warm but informational. Brevity still rules.
- **Support response**: Maximum warmth. Maximum patience. Specific solutions.
- **Legal text**: Different rules apply — accuracy first, plain language where possible, voice principles relaxed for compliance.

## Quick guide

| Audience | Default register |
|----------|------------------|
| Healthcare consumer | Warm-direct, professional, peer-leaning |
| B2B enterprise | Mid-formal, confident, expert-leaning |
| Emotional consumer | Warm-conversational, evocative |
| Developer / technical | Direct, precise, peer |
| Institutional | Formal, clear, expert |

## Common failure modes this tree prevents

- **Voice doesn't match audience** — formal in casual context, casual in formal context
- **Voice doesn't match product** — promises one thing, product does another
- **Trying to please everyone** — voice tries to be all registers, becomes no register
- **Surface-blind voice** — same exact register across in-app, marketing, support — when calibration would serve better
