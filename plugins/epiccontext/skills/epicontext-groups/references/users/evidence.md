# Evidence Library — Users Group

A library of cited data points from trusted sources (NN/g, McKinsey, Christensen Institute, Google + Behavioural Architects, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, POPAI, peer-reviewed research) that the entity references draw on when an argument benefits from supporting evidence.

**Principle: only data from a trusted source, only when actually available.** No filler. If an entity reference makes a claim and there's no trusted source to back it, the claim is omitted rather than padded with weaker material.

Each entry has an ID (e.g., `EVD-USR-001`). Entity references cite by ID rather than duplicating data inline.

## Sample size and research methodology

### EVD-USR-001 — 5 users find ~85% of usability problems
- **Claim:** With 5 users in a qualitative usability test, you uncover approximately 85% of usability problems available for discovery in that test iteration, assuming each problem has a discovery probability (L) of ~31%.
- **Source:** Nielsen, J. & Landauer, T.K. (1993). *A Mathematical Model of the Finding of Usability Problems*. Confirmed and republished by NN/g.
- **Citation:** [NN/g — How Many Test Users in a Usability Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/)
- **Caveats:** Applies only to **qualitative** usability testing. Does NOT apply to quantitative studies, card sorting, eye tracking, or interviews. The 85% figure is contingent on problem discoverability rate.
- **Used in:** research, journey-map

### EVD-USR-002 — Sample sizes by method
- **Claim (NN/g recommendations):**
  - Qualitative usability test: 5 users (per user group)
  - Quantitative study: at least 20 users for statistical significance
  - Card sorting: at least 15 users per user group
  - Eye tracking: 39 users for stable heatmaps
  - Interviews: 5 users often not enough; saturation point is method- and topic-dependent
  - FDA validation testing for medical devices: ~25 participants per user group
- **Source:** NN/g, Sauro & Lewis (2012), FDA Guidance (2011)
- **Citation:** [NN/g — How Many Test Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/) | [NN/g — How Many Participants for a UX Interview](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interview-sample-size/)
- **Used in:** research

### EVD-USR-003 — Five users find common problems, not all problems
- **Claim:** Five users will uncover ~91% of the **most common** problem tier (problems experienced by >30% of users), but only ~22% of the **least common** issues (those affecting <5% of users).
- **Source:** MeasuringU analysis of empirical data
- **Citation:** [MeasuringU — Sample Size in Usability Studies](https://measuringu.com/sample-size-reality/)
- **Used in:** research, journey-map

### EVD-USR-004 — Average company over-tests by 2x
- **Claim:** A NN/g UX Conference survey (n=217) found the average company runs 11 participants per round of usability testing — more than twice the recommended size for qualitative work.
- **Source:** NN/g, Jakob Nielsen
- **Citation:** [NN/g — How Many Test Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/)
- **Implication:** Argues for *more frequent, smaller* studies rather than fewer large ones.
- **Used in:** research

## Personas — usage and impact

### EVD-USR-005 — Persona update frequency correlates with impact
- **Claim:** NN/g's research indicates frequent updates to personas correlate with higher stakeholder ratings of personas' impact on the product's user experience.
- **Source:** NN/g
- **Citation:** [NN/g — Personas Are Living Documents](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/personas-are-living-documents/)
- **Used in:** persona

### EVD-USR-006 — Proto persona workshop format
- **Claim:** Proto persona workshops typically run 2–4 hours; each participant creates 2–5 proto personas; group discussion combines into a final set of 3–6.
- **Source:** NN/g
- **Citation:** [NN/g — 3 Persona Types](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona-types/)
- **Caveat:** Proto personas are assumption-based, not research-based.
- **Used in:** persona

### EVD-USR-007 — Recommended persona count per project
- **Claim:** NN/g recommends 3–6 personas per defined design context.
- **Source:** NN/g
- **Citation:** [NN/g — Just-Right Personas](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/persona-scope/)
- **Used in:** persona

## Journey mapping — value and impact

### EVD-USR-008 — Journeys outperform touchpoints as a CX metric
- **Claim:** Best-in-class companies optimize customer journeys, not just touchpoints. Customers experience companies through end-to-end experiences. Individual touchpoints may perform well even if the overall experience is poor.
- **Source:** McKinsey & Company
- **Citation:** [McKinsey — Customer Experience Insights](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/customer-experience-insights)
- **Used in:** journey-map

### EVD-USR-009 — CX leaders outperform laggards on revenue
- **Claim:** McKinsey analysis (US, 2016–2021): companies that are leaders in customer experience achieved more than 2x the revenue growth of "CX laggards." Companies executing experience-led growth deliver 30% higher TRS.
- **Source:** McKinsey & Company
- **Citation:** [McKinsey — Growth through Customer Experience](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/experience-led-growth-a-new-way-to-create-value)
- **Used in:** journey-map, customer-insight

### EVD-USR-010 — Journeys are repeatable
- **Claim:** Customer journeys are often longer than expected (e.g., cable industry onboarding extends through 2–3 billing cycles) and are repeatable for a meaningful percentage of customers.
- **Source:** McKinsey & Company
- **Citation:** [McKinsey — From Touchpoints to Journeys](https://www.mckinsey.de/~/media/mckinsey/industries/public%20and%20social%20sector/our%20insights/cx%20compendium%202017/customer-experience-compendium-july-2017.pdf)
- **Used in:** journey-map

## Customer insights and Voice of Customer

### EVD-USR-011 — CX surveys have major limitations
- **Claim:** McKinsey research finds typical CX surveys sample only ~7% of a company's customers; only 16% of CX leaders say surveys provide granular-enough data; only 4% say their system lets them calculate ROI of CX decisions.
- **Source:** McKinsey & Company
- **Citation:** [McKinsey — Prediction: The Future of CX](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/prediction-the-future-of-cx)
- **Used in:** customer-insight, research

### EVD-USR-012 — Bad CX has measurable cost
- **Claim:** PwC 2025 CX Survey: 52% of consumers stopped buying from a brand because of a bad experience. 56% rarely or never complain about bad service.
- **Source:** PwC, Renascence (cited in industry CX reports)
- **Citation:** Industry-aggregated; PwC original at PwC.com (2025 CX Survey)
- **Caveat:** Aggregated stats; treat as directional.
- **Used in:** customer-insight

## Jobs to Be Done

### EVD-USR-014 — JTBD origin and outcome data
- **Claim:** Tony Ulwick conceptualized JTBD/ODI in 1990 applying Six Sigma to innovation. First major application: Cordis Corporation's angioplasty balloon line. Cordis market share grew from 1% to 20%+; stock price more than quadrupled.
- **Source:** Strategyn / Tony Ulwick
- **Citation:** [Strategyn — Jobs to Be Done](https://strategyn.com/jobs-to-be-done/)
- **Caveat:** Single-company case; Strategyn is a primary source with commercial interest. Treat as illustrative.
- **Used in:** jtbd

### EVD-USR-015 — Christensen's three dimensions
- **Claim:** Every job has three dimensions — functional, emotional, social. Christensen's research finds products winning on functional alone tend to lose to products that address all three.
- **Source:** Christensen, *Competing Against Luck* (2016)
- **Citation:** Christensen Institute — [Jobs to Be Done Theory](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)
- **Used in:** jtbd

### EVD-USR-016 — Forces of Progress
- **Claim:** Progress is governed by four competing forces: Push, Pull, Anxiety, Habit. Switching only occurs when push + pull > anxiety + habit.
- **Source:** Christensen / Moesta & Spiek
- **Citation:** [Christensen Institute — JTBD Theory](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)
- **Used in:** jtbd, competitor

## Decision-making behavior (Google research)

### EVD-USR-019 — The Messy Middle of purchase behavior
- **Claim:** Consumer decision-making between trigger and purchase is non-linear. Buyers loop through **exploration** (expansive) and **evaluation** (reductive) modes repeatedly until they commit.
- **Source:** Google + The Behavioural Architects
- **Citation:** [Think with Google — Decoding Decisions: The Messy Middle of Purchase Behavior](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9998/Decoding_Decisions_The_Messy_Middle_of_Purchase_Behavior.pdf) | [Marketing in the messy middle (2023)](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-emea/consumer-insights/consumer-journey/the-consumer-decision-making-process/)
- **Methodology:** Multi-year research combining literature reviews, shopping observations, search trend analysis, and a large-scale shopping experiment.
- **Used in:** journey-map, customer-insight

### EVD-USR-020 — Six cognitive biases shape decisions
- **Claim:** Google identified six biases that disproportionately influence decisions: category heuristics, power of now, social proof, scarcity bias, authority bias, power of free.
- **Source:** Google + The Behavioural Architects (Rennie & Protheroe)
- **Citation:** [Think with Google — Decoding Decisions](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9998/Decoding_Decisions_The_Messy_Middle_of_Purchase_Behavior.pdf)
- **Used in:** persona, journey-map, customer-insight

## Buying behavior (empirical marketing science)

### EVD-USR-023 — Mental and Physical Availability drive brand growth
- **Claim:** Brands grow primarily by being **easy to mind** (mental availability) and **easy to find** (physical availability). Loyalty programs, hyper-targeting, and differentiation contribute much less to growth than these two factors.
- **Source:** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (Byron Sharp, Jenni Romaniuk)
- **Citation:** Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010); Romaniuk, *Better Brand Health* (2023). [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute](https://marketingscience.info/) | [LinkedIn B2B Institute — How B2B Brands Grow](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/b2b-institute/how-b2b-brands-grow)
- **Methodology:** Decades of empirical analysis across thousands of brands and categories, single-source panel data, replication.
- **Caveat:** Critics note the model works strongly in low-involvement FMCG categories but is incomplete for high-involvement, identity-driven, or high-emotional categories.
- **Used in:** persona, jtbd, competitor

### EVD-USR-024 — Category Entry Points (CEPs)
- **Claim:** Mental availability is built through associations between a brand and **Category Entry Points** — the cues, situations, motivations, and contexts that prompt buyers to think of the category.
- **Source:** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (Jenni Romaniuk)
- **Citation:** Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018); *Better Brand Health* (2023).
- **Implication:** JTBD circumstances ARE Category Entry Points. The same conceptual entity, two frameworks naming it.
- **Used in:** jtbd, persona

### EVD-USR-025 — Double Jeopardy Law
- **Claim:** Smaller brands suffer twice — they have fewer buyers AND those buyers are slightly less loyal. Growth comes primarily from acquiring new customers (penetration), not from increasing loyalty.
- **Source:** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (Jenni Romaniuk, John Dawes, Sahar Faghidno)
- **Citation:** [LinkedIn B2B Institute — How B2B Brands Grow: The Double Jeopardy Law](https://business.linkedin.com/marketing-solutions/b2b-institute/how-b2b-brands-grow)
- **Used in:** competitor

## In-store and offline behavior

### EVD-USR-026 — In-store decisions and unplanned purchases
- **Claim:** A significant share of purchase decisions in physical retail are made in-store rather than pre-planned. POPAI's Shopper Engagement Studies (2012, 2014) found ~76% of purchase decisions in mass merchant channels were made in-store. Eye-tracking and EEG show displays, packaging, and shelf design measurably influence the decision moment.
- **Source:** POPAI Shopper Engagement Study (with Sands Research, SmartRevenue, Eye Faster)
- **Citation:** [POPAI 2014 Mass Merchant Shopper Engagement Study](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hub/73834/file-1640923392-pdf/docs/popai_-_2014_mass_merchant_shopper_engagement_study.pdf) | [NMSBA — In-store Neuro Research](https://www.nmsba.com/news/212-in-store-neuro-research)
- **Methodology:** 2,991 shoppers across 13 supermarket retailers and 17 metropolitan markets; intercept interviews, eye-tracking (n=204), and EEG.
- **Caveat:** US grocery / mass merchant context; numbers will differ in service businesses, specialty retail, or other regions.
- **Used in:** journey-map, customer-insight

### EVD-USR-027 — Path-to-purchase observation methodology
- **Claim:** Observational research in physical environments — tracking software, heatmaps, eye-tracking, ethnographic observation — reveals patterns invisible to interview or survey methods. Stated reasons often diverge from observed behavior.
- **Source:** Underhill (1999) *Why We Buy*; peer-reviewed research using Shopper Flow©
- **Citation:** [PMC — The Use of Observational Technology to Study In-Store Behavior](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6701250/) | Underhill, *Why We Buy: The Science of Shopping*
- **Used in:** research, journey-map

### EVD-USR-028 — Empirical patterns of in-store behavior
- **Claim:** Peer-reviewed research using Ehrenberg's empirical generalizations approach has identified consistent patterns in shopper behavior across categories and stores: store coverage relates to time and intent, repeat visits create habituation patterns, brand-switching at retail follows NBD-Dirichlet distributions.
- **Source:** Sorensen et al., peer-reviewed work in *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*
- **Citation:** [ScienceDirect — Fundamental patterns of in-store shopper behavior](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969698916303186)
- **Used in:** persona, journey-map

### EVD-USR-029 — Just Enough Research as practitioner standard
- **Pointer:** Erika Hall's framework for matching research to question and team capacity. Core principles: research is for reducing uncertainty, not generating data; the right method is the one that answers the question with the least effort; bad research (leading questions, biased samples, opinion-as-fact) is worse than no research because it manufactures false confidence.
- **Citation:** Hall, E. (2019). *Just Enough Research* (2nd ed.). A Book Apart. ISBN 978-1-952616-08-6.
- **Used in:** research, which-research-method

### EVD-USR-030 — Indi Young qualitative depth and listening sessions
- **Pointer:** Methodology distinguishing "behavioral audiences" from demographic personas; uses 60-90 min non-leading listening sessions to map users' inner thinking — what they're trying to accomplish, the reasoning they apply, the reactions they have, the principles they hold. Synthesizes into "thinking style" segmentation.
- **Citation:** Young, I. (2015). *Practical Empathy*. Rosenfeld Media. Young, I. (2022). *Time to Listen*. Indi Young Books.
- **Caveat:** Time-intensive; pays off for strategic discovery, not tactical iteration.
- **Used in:** research, persona, which-research-method

### EVD-USR-031 — Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres)
- **Claim:** Teresa Torres's framework: product teams should make discovery a habit, not a project. Core practices: weekly customer touchpoints (every product trio member talks to a customer each week), Opportunity Solution Trees (outcome → opportunities → solutions → assumption tests), and assumption mapping (desirability, viability, feasibility, usability) as the way to test ideas before building. Continuous Discovery is the most-cited modern alternative to project-based research cycles.
- **Source:** Teresa Torres — Product Talk
- **Citation:** Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits: Discover Products that Create Customer Value and Business Value*. Product Talk LLC. ISBN 978-1736633304.
- **Caveat:** "Weekly customer contact" is an aspiration; teams under shipping pressure often fail to maintain it. Torres herself acknowledges that establishing the habit is the hardest part — the methodology assumes leadership has bought in.
- **Used in:** research, research-vs-ship, jtbd

### EVD-USR-032 — Steve Portigal interview craft
- **Claim:** Portigal's *Interviewing Users* establishes the practitioner standard for craft of user interviewing: how to plan an interview, types of questions to ask (and avoid), how to manage rapport and digressions, how to recognize signal vs. noise. Particularly strong on the techniques that beginner researchers get wrong (leading questions, confirmation-seeking, killing the conversation by talking too much). The book is widely used in UX bootcamps and design programs.
- **Source:** Steve Portigal — Portigal Consulting; Rosenfeld Media
- **Citation:** Portigal, S. (2023). *Interviewing Users: How to Uncover Compelling Insights* (2nd ed.). Rosenfeld Media. ISBN 978-1933820682.
- **Used in:** research, which-research-method

## Sources held for other groups

**AI search visibility / AEO research (Yext, Semrush, Stanford HAI, SE Ranking, Searchable)** — Belongs in the Performance or Brand group rather than Users. AEO is about how a brand gets found by AI, not about understanding user behavior. When building those groups' evidence libraries, cite primary sources directly rather than aggregator platforms.

## What's NOT in this library

- Vendor blog posts unless they cite a primary source
- Single-company case studies presented as general truth
- LinkedIn thought-leadership without underlying data
- AI-generated statistics
- Numbers without a clear primary source
